TailTag: Trap-Free eBPF Attribution of OS-Induced Tail Latency to Individual Requests Yashvanth S Jeevanandham T Nithillakrishi PY Akula Sai Teja CS24I1029 CS24I1035 CS24I1039 CS24I1054 Abstract—Slow requests in latency-critical services are often slowed by the operating system rather than the application, yet standard eBPF tools only report aggregate scheduler and interrupt statistics. We present TailTag, an eBPF pipeline that keeps per-thread preemption, wakeup, blocking and interrupt accounting in an mmap-able BPF map. The application snap- shots it at request boundaries with plain memory loads, with no trap or ring-buffer record per request. TailTag costs 122–220 ns of tracer CPU per request, 47–53× less than uprobe-based windows, and closes an accounting identity within 5 µs for at least 97.8% of requests. Across 291 runs of three workloads, a queue-length control explains most tails in the training workloads, and there aligned kernel features do no better than aggregate counts. On a held-out workload, however, aligned features keep PR-AUC 0.50 where aggregate counts fall to 0.26. Most of the signal is OS delay inherited from requests queued ahead. One-at-a-time interventions confirm 7 of 9 pre-stated signatures, and the two misses expose an interrupt path we had not predicted. We also show how hypervisor noise distorts VM-based tail studies, and contribute a runqlat patch to bcc. Index Terms—eBPF, tail latency, Linux scheduler, softirq, observability, attribution I. Introduction Domain. Interactive services are judged by their slowest requests. When one user action fans out to many servers, the 99th-percentile latency of a single server becomes close to the median latency the user sees [1]. Many of these slow requests are not caused by the application at all. The operating system delays them: a runnable thread waits behind another task, a burst of network softirqs steals the core in the middle of a request, a thread is migrated, or a write blocks on the storage stack. Li et al. [2] showed that such OS and hardware effects can stretch the tail of simple Linux servers by an order of magnitude beyond what queueing theory predicts. eBPF makes it possible to observe these events in production at low cost. Small verified programs attach to stable kernel tracepoints and summarize what they see in kernel maps [3]. The standard eBPF tools, however, report aggregates: a runqueue-latency histogram for a process, softirq time per CPU, counts per second [4]. An operator who sees a p99 spike and a rise in context switches in the same minute still cannot say whether the switches caused the slow requests, which requests they hit, or whether the requests were simply queued behind a load burst. Problem. Given a service with measurable per-request latency, we want to (i) detect tail requests, (ii) attach to each one the kernel events that delayed it, and (iii) determine which kernel indicators actually separate tail requests from normal ones. The tracing itself must add less than 5% overhead, and the method must not claim causality from correlation alone. Three things make this hard. Requests last tens of microseconds, so even two kernel traps per request are expensive. A request is often delayed by events that happened to the requests ahead of it in the queue. And on a virtualized test machine, the host can stall vCPUs in ways the guest kernel cannot observe. Contributions. • TailTag, an eBPF pipeline that keeps per-thread scheduler and IRQ accounting (preemption wait, wakeup delay, blocked time, interrupt time charged to the interrupted thread) in an mmap-able BPF map. (a mechanism ghost-bpf uses for scheduling [5]; we apply it to attribution). The application reads it at request boundaries with plain memory loads, so no trap or ring buffer record is needed per request. It costs 122–220 ns of tracer CPU per request, 47–53× less than uprobe-based windows (§IV-B). • A per-request latency decomposition that includes OS delay inherited from queued-ahead requests, checked by an accounting identity that holds within 5 µs for at least 97.8% of requests in every run. • An evaluation over three workloads, three loads and five repetitions. It compares aligned per-request fea- tures against aggregate event counts and a queueing- only control, including a workload held out from training (H1), and validates the attribution with one- at-a-time interventions (H2). • Two practical outcomes: a noise gate that exposes hypervisor interference in VM-based latency studies, and an upstream patch to bcc’s runqlat that separates wakeup latency from preemption latency. Hypotheses. H1: kernel telemetry aligned to request win- dows separates tail requests from normal ones better than aggregate event counts. H2: each controlled intervention moves the matching component of the attribution, and only that component. II. Related Work Sources of tail latency. Dean and Barroso [1] showed that, in fan-out services, the latency tail of individual servers sets the latency users see, and that rare stalls which hardly matter on one machine dominate at scale. Li et al. [2] is the study closest to ours. They compared null-RPC, Memcached and Nginx servers on Linux with an ideal queueing model and traced the excess tail to back- ground processes, non-FIFO scheduling, how connections are bound to threads, where NIC interrupts land relative to server threads, and power saving. Each cause was confirmed by changing one setting and rerunning; our one- at-a-time interventions follow the same idea. Treadmill [6] showed that load testers distort tails through client-side queueing, closed-loop arrivals and wrong aggregation. It then attributed tail changes to hardware and software factors with quantile regression over many runs. Its pitfalls changed our own harness (§III). Both works attribute the tail to a configuration, offline and across runs. They do not say which OS events delayed a given request while the system runs. Waiting and scheduler analysis. wPerf [7] records wait- ing events between threads and builds wait-for graphs to find the events that cap throughput, at about 5% overhead. Lozi et al. [8] found Linux load-balancing bugs that left cores idle while threads waited in runqueues, a direct case of the scheduler creating delay. Both target throughput or scheduler correctness, not single slow re- quests. eBPF observability. The kernel BPF interface [3] offers verified programs on stable tracepoints, maps and ring buffers. The bcc tools [4] build on it: runqlat reports runqueue latency as a histogram per CPU, process or cgroup, and similar tools cover softirq and off-CPU time. Netflix uses runqueue latency per container in production to detect noisy neighbours [9]. All of these aggregate over time or over a task, so their output cannot be joined with the latency of one request, and runqlat merges wakeup and preemption waits into one histogram. Recording every scheduler event instead, as perf sched or ftrace do, is complete but expensive: our own per-event streaming mode costs 63–104× more tracer CPU per request than in-kernel accounting (§IV-B). Request tracing and scheduling. Hindsight [10] keeps cheap trace data and retrieves it only for edge cases such as slow requests, but its spans stop at the application boundary, so scheduler and interrupt time inside a span stay invisible. Caladan [11] uses queueing delay as a fast control signal to reallocate cores at microsecond scale. Our finding that queue length is the strongest tail predictor agrees with that choice, but Caladan replaces the Linux scheduler with its own runtime. ghOSt [12] delegates Linux scheduling policy to user space, and its BPF extension shares a per-thread status table with user-space agents through an mmap-able BPF array [5]. We reuse that mechanism for a different purpose: attributing latency rather than making scheduling decisions. Gaps. We found no low-overhead mechanism that, on an unmodified Linux kernel, tags each request with the scheduler and interrupt delay it (and the requests queued ahead of it) suffered, compares that tag against aggregate event counts and a queueing-only control, tests generalization to an unseen workload, and validates the attribution with one-at-a-time interventions. That is the gap this work addresses. III. Methodology A. Testbed and workloads All experiments run in an Ubuntu 24.04 VM (Linux 6.8.0, 4 vCPUs, 6 GiB) under the macOS Virtualization framework on an Apple M3 Pro. Worker threads are re- stricted to CPUs 0–2, and the load generator and collector are pinned to CPU 3. Our benchmark, tlbench, generates open-loop Poisson arrivals with a fixed seed. Every request carries a stable integer id and is served by one of three workers. Three workload classes share this path: cpu (100 µs of fixed computation, woken through a condition variable); kv (8 lookups in a 64 MiB hash table, a 4 KiB pread, 20 µs of computation, and a pwrite+fdatasync every 200 requests); and net (loopback TCP to epoll- driven server threads, 50 µs of work, 12 connections). Each class runs at three loads (cpu 7/12/17k, kv 30/50/70k, net 10/17/24k req/s), which keep worker utilization be- tween 17% and 57%. The generator busy-waits instead of sleeping: in our pilot, a sleeping generator woke late on an idle vCPU and produced late, bursty arrivals, which is the pitfall Treadmill describes [6]. Latency is therefore measured from actual injection to completion, and generator lateness is logged separately. Noise gate. Host preemption of vCPUs is invisible to the guest. A meter, hiccup, spins one thread per vCPU and records gaps in its own execution. A run starts only when every vCPU loses less than 5% of its time (3% for the first eight runs), and the measurements before and after each run are stored with it. B. Kernel telemetry TailTag attaches BTF-typed tracepoint programs to sched_switch, sched_wakeup[_new], sched_migrate_task, softirq_entry/exit and irq_handler_entry/exit. For each thread of the target process, a small state machine keeps cumulative counters: (i) preemption wait, from a switch-out while still runnable (prev_state=TASK_RUNNING) to the next switch-in; (ii) wakeup delay, from sched_wakeup to switch-in; (iii) blocked time, from a voluntary switch-out to the wakeup; (iv) on-CPU time; (v) hard and soft IRQ time charged to the interrupted thread, which is the current task in IRQ context; and (vi) counts of voluntary and involuntary switches, migrations and IRQs. Wakeups of a task that is already running or queued are ignored. Every program filters on the TGID first, so other tasks cost one comparison. All programs also update system- wide per-CPU counters (switches, involuntary switches, wakeups, migrations, hardirqs, softirqs per vector), which the collector samples every 5 ms together with PSI. This is the aggregate event-count baseline. C. Joining requests to kernel state We compare three ways to align a request window with the counters. Uprobe windows attach uprobes to two empty functions, tl_req_begin/tl_req_end. The begin probe snapshots the thread’s counters in the kernel, and the end probe emits one 120-byte record of differ- ences through a ring buffer. This needs no change to the application but costs two traps per request. Per- event streaming sends every matching scheduler and IRQ event to user space for offline joining. Shared counters, the design we propose, keep the per-thread state in a BPF_F_MMAPABLE array (4096 slots, indexed by tid mod 4096, with the owning tid checked). The appli- cation maps the pinned array and snapshots its own slot with ordinary loads at request begin and end. Because bpf_ktime_get_ns and CLOCK_MONOTONIC are the same clock, the running on-CPU segment can be closed in user space. No trap, syscall or record happens per request; kernel work happens only when a scheduler or IRQ event actually affects a target thread. Validation. For each request we check the identity t end − t start = oncpu + runq + wake + blocked , whose four terms come from different tracepoints. D. Per-request decomposition Latency is split into: sched (wakeup delay before service, plus preemption and wakeup waits during service); irq; blocked; exec (on-CPU time minus IRQ time); and queue (waiting for a worker). A request queued behind a request that was preempted is late because of an OS event it never saw itself. We therefore compute inherited delay: for request i, the significant OS delay (above 10 µs per request, so that many small delays cannot add up to a proxy for queue length) of all requests that completed during i’s queue wait, divided by the number of workers. The queue term is split into inherited sched/irq/blocked parts, capped at the wait, and a remaining load part. A tail request is attributed to the component with the largest excess over the median of non-tail requests in the same run. E. Detection and attribution analysis A tail request is one whose latency exceeds the median p99 of five separate calibration runs (seeds 1000–1004, never evaluated) for the same workload and load. Features are the 11 per-window counters plus the 3 inherited terms. Durations are divided by the tail threshold so that values are comparable across workloads, and all features are log(1+x) transformed. We compare an interpretable rule (total OS delay > τ ), logistic regression and a random forest (150 trees, depth 10), each on aligned features, on aggregate counts over 5/20/100 ms windows, and on a queueing-only control (requests completed during the wait). Models are trained on complete cpu/kv runs (repetitions 0–2) and tested on unseen repetitions (3– 4) and on the net workload, which is never used for training or feature choice. We report PR-AUC with run- level bootstrap 95% CIs, precision, recall and F1 at the F1-optimal training threshold, and false alarms per minute. For memory reasons we keep 5% of normal and at most 20 000 tail requests per run, with inverse-probability weights in every metric. Interventions (H2). At mid load we add one stressor at a time, five repetitions each, in randomized order: a bursty CPU hog on the worker CPUs (40% duty per 10 ms); a softirq flood (UDP over loopback sent from CPU 3, with RPS steering receive processing to CPUs 0–2); an I/O writer (1 MiB writes with fdatasync); and an aﬀinity change (a hog on CPU 0 with workers on CPUs 0–2, versus workers moved to CPUs 1–2 at 2/3 of the rate, so that per-CPU load is equal). For each stressor we state the expected signature in advance (hog → sched, flood → irq, I/O → blocked, moving the workers → less sched). We then test it against the control with a one-sided Mann– Whitney U test on the per-run mean of that delay per request. IV. Experimental Results We ran 291 runs: 45 calibration (5 per condition), 90 main, 62 overhead, 70 intervention, 16 A/B and 8 receiver-placement runs. All passed the noise gate. Median platform noise was 0.6% of vCPU time. A. Measurement validity In all 115 traced runs every request received kernel features (coverage 100.00%), there were no slot collisions, and 0 events were lost. The accounting identity closed within 5 µs for at least 97.8% of requests in every run (median residual 167 ns, which is the distance between the timestamp and the slot snapshot). Generator lateness was below 60 µs at p99 in 73 of 90 main runs; the worst run reached 24 ms. Platform collapse episodes. A minority of runs, traced or not, contain episodes of a few hundred milliseconds in which the queue grows to tens of milliseconds or more. These episodes make the shelves in Fig. 1. In the worst kv run, the median service time doubled (22.5→49.8 µs) for 600 ms with no scheduler, IRQ or blocking events: on-CPU time simply got longer. That is consistent with the host slowing the vCPU, which the guest cannot observe. Other episodes show neither. A single episode in a calibration run raised its p99 to 20 ms for cpu-mid, against a five- run median of 207 µs, so we use the median as the tail threshold. B. Threats to validity All results come from one 4-vCPU VM, one host and one kernel (6.8). Host preemption and slowdown of vCPUs are invisible to the guest. The noise gate bounds them, but they remain the likely source of unattributed collapse episodes. The workloads are synthetic. The inheritance rule assumes a shared FIFO queue and does not model per- socket queues (net). Test sets include stressed runs, which raises their tail rate (base rate 20%). Shared counters need two calls in the application; unmodified binaries must use uprobe windows. V. Conclusion TailTag shows that per-request attribution of OS- induced delay does not require a trap per request. Per- thread accounting kept by tracepoint programs and read through a shared BPF map costs about as much as plain event counters, and its accounting closes. The evaluation gives a more nuanced answer than our hypothesis. Within a known workload, queueing explains most tail requests, and any signal that tracks load (including aggregate counts) detects them. The value of request-aligned kernel telemetry shows up in two places: it generalizes to a workload not seen during training, and it names the cause. Controlled interventions moved exactly the predicted delay component in seven of nine cases. The remaining cases, together with the client-side receiver delay of the net workload, show where the method stops: OS delay on threads outside the request window, and host effects invisible to the guest. Future work is to extend windows across all threads a request touches, to model per-socket queues, and to repeat the study on bare metal. As a by-product, we prepared a patch that lets bcc’s runqlat separate wakeup latency from preemption latency, the distinction our intervention results relied on. References [1] J. Dean and L. A.
