\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}

We ran 336 runs: 45 calibration, 90 main, 62 overhead, 70 intervention,
16 A/B, 8 receiver-placement, and 45 added after review (36 validation,
9 fresh net). The noise gate waits for a quiet host instead of discarding
runs; of the first 318 runs, \nwWaited{} had to wait (median
\nwMed\,s, longest \nwMax\,min). Median platform noise was \noiseMed\% of
vCPU time.

\subsection{Are the measurements correct?}
In all \valRuns{} traced runs every request received kernel data (coverage
\valCoverage\%), no two threads shared a slot, and \valDrops{} events were
lost. Equation~\eqref{eq:identity} held within 5\,$\mu$s for at least
\valWithin\% of requests in every run (median error 167\,ns). For a request
that never leaves the CPU this is nearly automatic; the real test is the
\idCampSwShare\% of requests that were switched out during their window.
For those, the identity held for \idCampSwMed\% of requests in the median
run but only \idCampSwMin\% in the worst. Part of that gap was a bug: the
application read the on-CPU timestamp and total as two separate loads, so a
switch between them was counted twice. With a retry, 18 validation runs
reached at least \idFixOneSwMin\%. A reviewer also found that our
switch-out test ignored the kernel's \texttt{preempt} flag. After the fix,
a rerun saw none of \preAll{} preemptions hit a thread that was about to
sleep (this kernel preempts only at safe points), so the earlier data are
unaffected. The generator was less than 60\,$\mu$s late at p99 in \genOk{}
of \genAll{} main runs; the worst run was \genMax{} late.

\textbf{Baseline latency.} Untraced at mid load, p50/p95/p99 are
\latCpuMidPFifty/\latCpuMidPNinetyFive/\latCpuMidPNinetyNine\,$\mu$s (cpu),
\latKvMidPFifty/\latKvMidPNinetyFive/\latKvMidPNinetyNine\,$\mu$s (kv) and
\latNetMidPFifty/\latNetMidPNinetyFive/\latNetMidPNinetyNine\,$\mu$s (net).
The p99 of identical runs varies widely (coefficient of variation
\cvMin--\cvMax), so we report medians of five runs. The attribution is
steadier: the share of tail latency given to each component varies across
the five runs of a condition by a median of \stabMed{} percentage points (at
most \stabMax, unstressed kv).

\textbf{Collapse episodes.} Some runs, traced or not, contain episodes of a
few hundred milliseconds in which the queue grows to tens of milliseconds
(shelves in Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}). In the worst kv run, the
service time doubled for 600\,ms with no scheduler, interrupt or blocking
events at all, which fits the host slowing down the vCPU. One such episode
raised a single calibration run's p99 to \thrMaxCpuMid{} (cpu-mid; five-run
median \thrCpuMid), which is why the threshold is a median of five runs.

\subsection{Detecting tail requests (H1)}
\label{sec:h1}
Table~\ref{tab:detect} and Fig.~\ref{fig:pr} show one limit and three
findings. \textbf{The limit:} \noSigTest\% of the test tail requests
(\noSigHeld\% on net) carry no OS-delay signal at all, i.e.\ no own or
inherited scheduler, interrupt or blocking delay of 10\,$\mu$s or more
(\noSigFloorFive--\noSigFloorTwenty\% of all tails for thresholds of
5--20\,$\mu$s). Their delay is pure queueing or host time, so no model built
on kernel data can rank them. This causes the sharp drop in precision near
0.45 recall in Fig.~\ref{fig:pr}.

\textbf{(1) Within a known workload, queueing explains most tails.} The
queue-length control reaches a PR-AUC of \apQLTest. It counts requests that
finished \emph{during} the wait, so it is only known afterwards: it tells us
how much of the tail is queueing, not how to predict it. All H1 features
are complete only when the request ends, so H1 measures diagnosis; only the
early-warning test below is a prediction. \textbf{(2) Within a known
workload, per-request kernel data are no better than aggregate counts}:
\apAlFTest{} vs.\ \apAgFTest{} (forest) and \apAlLTest{} vs.\ \apAgLTest{}
(logistic), and 5\,ms aggregates reach \ablAggFiveTest{}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}). \textbf{(3) On an unseen workload, the result
depends on where the tails come from.} With net held out (base rate
\baseHeld\%), aligned features reach about twice the PR-AUC of aggregates:
\apAlFHeld, \apAlLHeld{} and \apRuleHeld{} (forest, logistic, rule) against
\apAgFHeld, \apAgLHeld{} and \apAggRuleHeld. Holding out the other
workloads does not confirm this: for cpu the forest gives \rotCpuAlF{} vs.\
\rotCpuAgF{} but logistic regression \rotCpuAlL{} vs.\ \rotCpuAgL, and for
kv \rotKvAlF{} vs.\ \rotKvAgF{} and \rotKvAlL{} vs.\ \rotKvAgL. On
\freshRuns{} fresh net runs with new seeds and no stressor (base rate
\freshBasePct\%), aligned features fall to \freshAlL, below the \freshAgL{}
of aggregates. The reason is where the tails arise. In the held-out net
set, which includes the CPU-hog runs, only \noSigHeld\% of tails lack an OS
signal; in the fresh runs 69--99\% do, because they come from the client's
receive path (below). Per-request kernel data finds OS-caused tails in
unseen workloads, but not tails produced outside the request window, so
\textbf{H1 does not hold in general}. At the F1-optimal threshold, the
aligned forest raises \faKAlMin--\faKAlMax{}k false alarms per minute and
the aggregate forest \faKAgMin--\faKAgMax{}k, out of 0.4--4 million
requests per minute.

\textbf{Inherited delay matters.} Without it, held-out PR-AUC falls from
\ablAllHeld{} to \ablNoInhHeld: most slow requests were delayed by OS events
that hit the requests ahead of them, not themselves. Scheduler features
carry the most information (without them: \ablNoSchedHeld); interrupt and
blocking features add little on their own.

\textbf{Why net is hard.} Net tails arise on the client. Most of their
latency is on the response path ($t_\mathrm{done}-t_\mathrm{end}$), because
the client's receive thread shares CPU~3 with the spinning generator.
Moving the receiver to CPU~2 cut the response-path p99 from \rcvGenRespP{}
to \rcvOwnRespP, but total p99 stayed the same (\rcvGenP{} vs.\ \rcvOwnP)
because the server-side p99 rose to \rcvOwnServer: the receiver now
competes with a worker. The delay follows the co-located thread,
outside the request window.

\textbf{Early warning.} The kernel data of one 10\,ms window predicts
whether a tail episode starts in the next window with PR-AUC \ewApTest{}
(test) and \ewApHeld{} (net). This beats assuming the next window looks
like the current one (\ewPersTest, \ewPersHeld) and the base rate, the
fraction of windows followed by an episode (\ewBasePctTest\%,
\ewBasePctHeld\%).

\subsection{Controlled interventions (H2)}
\label{sec:h2}
Fig.~\ref{fig:signature} shows how each stressor changed each kind of
delay. \textbf{\hTwoRaw{} of \hTwoN{} predictions hold} at $p<0.05$
(one-sided Mann--Whitney test, 5 vs.\ 5 runs; with five runs per side the
smallest possible $p$ is 0.004), and \hTwoHolm{} still hold after Holm
correction for \hTwoN{} tests. The CPU hog raises the mean preemption wait
per request \sigCpuHogRunq$\times$ (cpu,
\absCpuHogRunqCtl$\to$\absCpuHogRunqInt\,$\mu$s), \sigKvHogRunq$\times$ (kv,
\absKvHogRunqCtl$\to$\absKvHogRunqInt\,$\mu$s) and \sigNetHogRunq$\times$
(net, \absNetHogRunqCtl$\to$\absNetHogRunqInt\,$\mu$s), and the wakeup delay
15--46$\times$; the ratios are large because the baseline is only tens of
nanoseconds. Interrupt and blocked time do not change. Moving the workers
off the hog's CPU cuts the preemption wait to \sigCpuMoveRunq$\times$ (cpu,
\absCpuMoveRunqCtl$\to$\absCpuMoveRunqInt\,$\mu$s) and \sigKvMoveRunq$\times$
(kv, \absKvMoveRunqCtl$\to$\absKvMoveRunqInt\,$\mu$s). The I/O writer raises
kv blocked time \sigKvIoBlk$\times$
(\absKvIoBlkCtl$\to$\absKvIoBlkInt\,$\mu$s) and interrupt time
\sigKvIoIrq$\times$. Two results are not significant after correction
($p{=}0.048$): moving the kv workers, and the softirq flood on cpu (only
+0.16\,$\mu$s of interrupt time per request).

The two misses are useful. The softirq flood had no clear effect on kv
(\sigKvFloodIrq$\times$ interrupt time): kv workers are often asleep, so the
steered softirqs mostly land on idle CPUs. For the cpu workload, which never
blocks, the I/O writer showed up as \sigCpuIoIrq$\times$ more
\emph{interrupt} time instead
(\absCpuIoIrqCtl$\to$\absCpuIoIrqInt\,$\mu$s), because the disk-completion
interrupts land on the worker CPUs. Our prediction (blocked time) missed
this cause, and only charging interrupt time to the interrupted thread
could reveal it.

\subsection{Overhead}
\label{sec:overhead}
In closed loop, throughput varied by up to $\pm$17\% between identical
untraced runs, so the differences in Fig.~\ref{fig:overhead} are within
noise. A steadier measure is the tracer's CPU time per request. Shared
counters cost \ovNsCpuShm{}\,ns (cpu) and \ovNsKvShm{}\,ns (kv), about as
much as counting events only. Uprobe windows cost
\ovNsKvAttrib--\ovNsCpuAttrib{}\,ns even before the traps themselves, and
per-event streaming up to \ovNsCpuStream{}\,ns: 47--53$\times$ and
63--104$\times$ the cost of shared counters. The BPF maps take
\memCnt\,MB (\memRb\,MB with the ring buffer), and no events were dropped.
In open loop, an interleaved A/B test (\abRuns{} untraced and \abRuns{}
traced runs, kv mid) was inconclusive: median p50 \abPfiftyNone{} vs.\
\abPfiftyShm{}\,$\mu$s ($p{=}\abPfiftyP$), median p99 \abPninetyNone{} vs.\
\abPninetyShm{} ($p{=}\abPninetyP$), and \abCollNone{} vs.\ \abCollShm{}
runs with a collapse episode (Fisher $p{=}\abCollP$), both on the traced
side. Eight runs per side rule out only large effects. The firmer evidence
for the 5\% budget is the cost per request: about 0.2\% (cpu) and 0.5\% (kv)
of the service time.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
We chose the 10\,$\mu$s threshold and the queue-length control on early
test data that included net runs, so net was not fully unseen while we
designed the features; the fresh net runs are the clean test, and there H1
failed. All results come from one 4-vCPU VM on one host with one kernel
(6.8). The guest cannot see the host pausing or slowing its vCPUs; the
noise gate limits this, but it remains the likely cause of the collapse
episodes we cannot explain. The workloads are synthetic. The
inherited-delay rule assumes one shared FIFO queue and does not model
per-connection queues (net). Hard interrupts nested inside a softirq are
counted twice, and on loopback a net thread's own receive processing counts
as interrupt time. Shared counters need two calls in the application;
unmodified programs must use uprobe windows.
